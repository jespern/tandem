Tandem is a protocol for keeping collections of records in sync across many devices and many people, end-to-end encrypted, in real time. It needs no server of its own. Its only infrastructure is a mailbox: one account on any IMAP server.

It is for people who trust each other. It does not work in an adversarial environment.

What is Tandem?

Every piece of data is an encrypted, signed message in one folder of a mail account. The mail server sees opaque ciphertext, sizes and timing. Nothing else.

Who can read a collection is decided by who holds its key. Who may change what is decided by signatures checked on every device. The mail server decides nothing.

Records merge without coordination, so devices work offline and in any order, and every device ends up with the same state.

Live updates use IMAP IDLE: the mail server notifies connected devices when messages arrive, without polling. Our Fastmail transport tests measured about 0.5 seconds for an appended message to reach another connected client. Other providers are slower; see which mail servers work. App batching, the network and the provider affect the time an edit takes to appear. On mobile, live updates run while the app is active; it catches up when reopened.

Tandem adds its own identity and key management above IMAP. Each person has a participant record within a hub, with independent signing and encryption keys for each approved device. Those devices share an encrypted home, so new lists and settings sync automatically. A password-protected hub management key approves new devices; each collection's owner controls sharing and its encryption keys. The mailbox credential alone gives no access to private contents.

Tandem was written for Liszto, a to-do app. It doesn't know that.

Who is it for?

A household, friends, a small team. People who trust each other.

Like a tandem bicycle, it only works when its riders cooperate: everyone is honest and wants to keep it moving forward.

Tandem does not work in an adversarial environment. Everyone who shares the mailbox holds its credential, and anyone who holds it can delete everything in it. A member who can write to a collection can vandalise it until the owner removes them. A stolen device can act for you until it is revoked; cutting off future access also requires fresh home and collection keys.

The encryption keeps the mail provider, and people on the mailbox who aren't members, from reading or forging your data. It does not protect you from the people you let in.

Why a mailbox?

Two words: no servers.

Three more: not ours, anyway. Most mail accounts work. There is no relay, no push service and no key server to run, and a mailbox you pay for is more predictable than infrastructure that shrinks and grows on other people's terms.

Keeping things that aren't mail in a mail account is not a new idea. Kolab did it. Apple Notes did it. See the prior art.

What can't it do?

Quite a lot.

  • Stop someone who holds the mailbox credential from deleting your data. They can't read it and they can't forge it, but they can delete it. Honest devices keep full copies and upload again what was destroyed.
  • Erase what a stolen device already downloaded. Its device keys can be revoked, and fresh keys protect future data after rekeying. Previously downloaded data remains readable.
  • Recover everyone's private data through the hub admin. An ordinary member who loses every device must rejoin and have lists shared again. Private data with no readable replica is lost. The hub recovery key can recover the creator's own access, not another member's home.
  • Choose between two conflicting histories of who a collection is shared with. If that ever happens the collection is dead. Same remedy.
  • Hide from the mail server how many messages you write, how big they are, or when.
  • Outlook.com. Personal Microsoft accounts only accept OAuth sign-ins, and the IMAP binding wants a password or an app password.

Not sorry

In Lean, sorry means “the proof goes here.” Our proofs have none.

This is a draft for review, not a security sign-off.

The mechanics are formally checked. Machine-checked proofs cover how edits merge, who may change who a list is shared with and what happens when two changes collide, cleaning up the mailbox without losing a write, sharing and answering, admitting, pairing and revoking devices, hub invitations and rejection receipts, keeping revoked devices out of the next home epoch, the directory, the hub password, and the byte encodings that get signed. The same definitions run as a test oracle, compared against an independent model on thousands of random histories.

The proofs take the cryptography as given: that ciphers, signatures and Argon2id do their job. Conformance tests check the real primitives, and mail-transport probes check real servers. The step-by-step procedures, such as rotating a key after a crash, and the mail binding itself are tested rather than proved. The complete wire format, a production implementation and an independent security review are still to come.

A tandem is a bicycle for two. It is also how a two-seat jet seats its crew: one behind the other. And the domain was available.
